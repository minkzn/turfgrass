Growing Point

  • The growing point at the top of the crown continually forms leaf primordia which eventually develops into fully expanded leaves (image above).
  • Visible Leaves The number of leaf primordia visible at any time varies from a few to as many as twenty or more, depending on species, plant age, and environmental conditions.

Turfgrass Stems

  • Indeterminate rhizomes are long and tend to branch at the nodes.
  • At the same time, new tillers also become evident as the leaves of the parent shoot are either pushed away or senesce.
  • Determinate rhizomes are relatively short; their growth occurs in three phases: initially downward, then horizontal, and finally upward.
  • While both seminal and adventitious roots (image on right) may be observed growing from turfgrass plants, the root system associated with a mature turfgrass community is expected to be entirely adventitious.

Roots

  • The root cap, which is unconnected to the root's vascular system, is composed of loosely arranged cells that are completely renewed every 6 to 9 days.
  • Root hairs measure about 100 microns (micrometers) in length, and 5 to 17 microns in diameter; their density is 50 to 100 per mm of root surface.
  • At each tip, this may involve the production of 18,000 new cells and extension of up to 5 cm per day.
  • The functions of the root cap include protecting the root tip, regulating the geotrophic curvature of the root as it grows, and producing a slime sheath.
Source: M3.5_ Roots_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

  • Water moves from soil into roots along a gradient of decreasing water potential.

Rhizosphere

  • The rhizosphere soil (i.e., ectorhizosphere) contains as many as 50 to 100 times as many microorganisms as bulk soil due to the release of exudate, lysates and mucilages by roots.

Stage 6 - Late-summer Recuperative Growth

  • It begins when daytime—and especially nighttime—temperatures cool, and annual bluegrass shoot and root growth becomes more vigorous.
  • Irrigation should be sufficient to sustain healthy growth and density.
  • The fertilization rate for nitrogen and other nutrients should be increased to compensate for the increased availability of carbohydrates and promote sufficient recuperative growth where the turf has been damaged.
  • As in Stage 4, this is an excellent time to hollow-tine cultivate, perhaps removing the cores, and topdressing to fill the holes.
Source: M5.10_ Stage 6 - Late-summer Recuperative Growth_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Stage 7 - Fall Hardening-off Period

  • Nitrogen applications should be avoided during this period; otherwise, the hardening off process, including the synthesis of cold-tolerance proteins, will be undermined.
  • Irrigation should be reduced or avoided, depending on temperatures and soil conditions.
  • Cold-tolerance proteins, which function as antifreeze to prevent ice crystals from forming within plant cells, are also synthesized during this stage.
  • The hardening-off process begins with the onset of cooler temperatures and shorter days, and continues through early winter.
Source: M5.11_ Stage 7 - Fall Hardening-off Period_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Stage 8 - Mid-fall Dormancy

  • The last snow-mold fungicide application should be made at this stage to ensure adequate preventive control of both Typhula blight and Microdochium patch.
  • In areas where continuous ice cover is likely, covers should be employed to protect the turf.
  • Some superintendents believe that this is a good time to cultivate in order to maximize the effects of freezing and thawing cycles for alleviating soil compaction.
Source: M5.12_ Stage 8 - Mid-fall Dormancy_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf

Cardinal Temperatures in Turfgrass Growth and Survival

  • Cool-season species, Kentucky bluegrass, the bentgrasses, the fescues, the ryegrasses, grow best in a temperature range roughly between 60 and 75°F, with growth slowing sharply above and below that window.
  • Shoot growth is typically greatest in spring and again in early fall, when air and soil temperatures sit within the optimal range for leaf and tiller development, and it declines sharply during summer heat even when moisture is adequate.
  • Through the C-4 cycle, warm-season species are able to more efficiently capture CO , resulting in much lower levels of photorespiration and, therefore, much higher rates of net photosynthesis.
  • Warm-season turfgrasses have a very different seasonal growth pattern.
Source: M5.2_ Cardinal Temperatures in Turfgrass Growth and Survival_ TURF 235 Fall 2026 (WC).pdf
